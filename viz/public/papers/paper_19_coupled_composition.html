Toward PK-Free Molecular Hamiltonians: Two-Center Sturmian Integrals and Coupled Composition

Josh Loutey · GeoVac Paper 19 · Quantum Chemistry · DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.21286728 · PDF

Abstract

The composed natural geometry framework (Paper 17) constructs molecular Hamiltonians by partitioning electrons into single-center blocks and coupling them via Phillips–Kleinman (PK) pseudopotentials. This produces structurally sparse qubit Hamiltonians—a Pauli count exactly linear in the qubit count across molecules at fixed basis, $N_{\mathrm{Pauli}} = 27.90 × Q$, with $51×$–$317×$ fewer terms than equal-qubit Gaussian baselines (Paper 14; the retired pair-diagonal rule gave $O(Q^{2.5})$ and 51–1{,}712$×$)—but PK limits accuracy to 5.3–19.4% equilibrium bond length error. PK has resisted improvement: six modification attempts have failed (v2.0.6–23), and the overcounting diverges at higher angular momentum. We report a negative result (Track CB, v2.0.37): replacing PK with explicit cross-block electron-electron integrals without corresponding cross-center nuclear attraction integrals produces an unbalanced Hamiltonian (29% error, worse than PK's 15%). The root cause is identified: two-center one-body integrals $\langle \psi_{nlm}^A | V_B | \psi_{n'l'm'}^A \rangle$, where an orbital on center $A$ feels the nuclear potential of center $B$, are required for energy balance but absent from the single-center framework. We observe that these missing integrals are closely related to the Shibuya–Wulfman integrals of the Coulomb Sturmian literature [ref], which are computed via Fock's momentum-space projection onto $S^3$—the same conformal map that underlies the GeoVac framework (Paper 7). The mathematical foundation is therefore already in place. We review the convergence properties of the Shibuya–Wulfman expansion, identify the Coulomb resolution technique [ref] as a potentially faster alternative, and outline the ``coupled composition'' architecture that would result from incorporating two-center integrals. If the angular expansion converges in few terms at molecular bond lengths, the coupled Hamiltonian would be PK-free, structurally sparse, and significantly more accurate than the current composed framework. This paper presents the balanced coupled architecture, documents its mathematical foundations, and reports computational benchmarks across three molecules (LiH, BeH$_2$, H$_2$O). We report computational results from the first three phases. The convergence concern is resolved: the multipole expansion of the cross-center potential terminates exactly at $L_{\max} = 2l_{\max}$ by Gaunt selection rules (33 one-body terms at $n_{\max}=2$). The balanced Hamiltonian has 2{,}726 Pauli terms (Conjecture 1 holds in its ratio form only; see Sec. (ref)). Four-electron FCI gives a PES minimum with $R_{\rm eq}$ error of 7.0%, the only minimum among the four coupling schemes tested: the other three have $R$-independent one-body and electron-repulsion integrals, so their energies are a constant plus $3/R$ and cannot bind. No dissociation energy is defined: the balanced electron repulsion is also built $R$-independent, and past the minimum the curve keeps rising instead of approaching the separated atoms (Conjecture 2 not confirmed in strict sense, but the comparison crosses FCI regimes). At $n_{\max} = 3$ ($Q = 84$), single-point FCI gives 0.20% energy error (down from 1.8%) with analytical integrals (incomplete gamma functions, machine precision), the error decreasing through $n_{\max}=3$; at $n_{\max}=4$ the model crosses past exact (over-binding), so the fixed-geometry error is not monotonically improvable either. The $R_{\rm eq}$ drift is structural to the block decomposition ($+0.053$ then $+0.023$ bohr at the two available steps— decelerating, not a constant rate; the PK solver's drift, $+0.30$–$0.37$ bohr per unit $l_{\max}$ in Paper 17, is in a different truncation parameter and is not directly comparable), not numerical. It is, however, not a physics wall: a companion all-electron prolate-spheroidal treatment with an $R$-accurate analytic atom-centered core resolves it to $R_{\rm eq} = +0.2%$ (the corpus's best LiH geometry) and, with a rigorous VMC-over-FCI treatment (a two-body Jastrow on the analytic-orbital FCI state), reaches a variational energy of $-8.047$ Ha—$15$ mHa below the $-8.032$ orbital ceiling and safely above the exact $-8.0705$. A deterministic, resolution-of-identity–free explicit-$r_{12}$ treatment on the same $π$-containing reference, with a cusp-correct Slater geminal, improves this to $-8.0490$ Ha (variational, cross-checked by VMC of the same trial function to $0.16$ mHa); relaxing the CI coefficients alongside the geminal lowers it to $-8.0516$ Ha, and adding electron–nucleus factors on the Li core, built from the core orbital itself with no new parameter, to $-8.0585$ Ha. An additive-F12 estimate places the near-chemical target at $\approx-8.062$ Ha; the remaining gap is not electron–electron–nucleus correlation, which is worth $\lesssim3$ mHa here. The atom-centered core turns out to be a convenience rather than a necessity: a pure prolate product basis also represents it, at the cost of hundreds of primitives. The prolate route buys energy per qubit, not sparsity. Its first qubit Hamiltonian ($Q=20$) has $8{,}370$ Pauli terms, level with an equal-size Gaussian set and ten times the composed count at $Q=30$, at an energy $19$ mHa better than that Gaussian set. Together these results locate the balanced drift specifically in the frozen/parameter-free core rather than in the coupled architecture itself.
